## 1. Principle and definitions
- [MIL-F001] Milling removes metal using a rotating multi-point cutting tool called a milling cutter. [LEC s.3; TA s.6]
- [MIL-F002] In milling the cutter rotates (at high speed) at a fixed position while the workpiece is fed against it. [NB p.1; TA s.6]
- [MIL-F003] The movement of the workpiece (table) towards the cutter is called the feed. [NB p.1]
- [MIL-F004] Each tooth of the cutter removes a small chip during every revolution. [TA s.6]
- [MIL-F005] The milling machine is one of the most versatile conventional machine tools; it can produce flat surfaces, slots, contours, gears and complex shapes. [LEC s.3; TA s.6]
- [MIL-F006] Milling cutters are generally made of high-speed steel (HSS). [LEC s.3]
- [MIL-F007] Machining is a subtractive process: excess material is removed to obtain the required geometry and finish. [TA s.5]

## 2. Up milling vs down milling
- [MIL-F008] Up milling is also called conventional milling; the cutter rotates against (opposite to) the direction of feed. [LEC s.4; NB p.1; TA s.8]
- [MIL-F009] In up milling the chip thickness is minimum (zero) at the start of the cut and maximum at the end. [LEC s.4; TA s.8]
- [MIL-F010] Down milling is also called climb milling; the cutter rotates in the same direction as the feed. [LEC s.6; NB p.1; TA s.8]
- [MIL-F011] In down milling the chip thickness is maximum at the start of the cut and minimum at the end. [LEC s.6; TA s.8]
- [MIL-F012] Up milling: scale or contamination on the work surface does not affect tool life much, so it is preferred for hard/scaled work and machines with backlash. [LEC s.5; TA s.8]
- [MIL-F013] Up milling disadvantages: the cutter tends to lift (pull up) the workpiece, so firm clamping is needed, and the tool tends to chatter. [LEC s.5]
- [MIL-F014] Up milling gives a rougher surface (rubbing at entry) and needs more power than down milling. [TA s.8]
- [MIL-F015] Down milling gives a better surface finish and puts less radial pressure on the arbor; it needs less power. [LEC s.7; TA s.8]
- [MIL-F016] Down milling is not suitable for workpieces having scale, because the scale reduces tool life. [LEC s.7]

## 3. Milling operations
- [MIL-F017] Plain (slab) milling produces a flat surface with teeth on the periphery of the cutter; the cutter axis is parallel to the work surface. [LEC s.9]
- [MIL-F018] A helical plain milling cutter (nick cutter) is used for slab milling. [NB p.1; CHART]
- [MIL-F019] Face milling uses a cutter whose axis is perpendicular to the work surface; it produces flat faces. [LEC s.10]
- [MIL-F020] End milling uses a cutter that rotates on an axis perpendicular to the work; with cutter diameter less than work width, it cuts slots. [LEC s.11]
- [MIL-F021] Straddle milling uses two side (side-and-face) cutters on one arbor, separated by spacing collars, to machine two parallel surfaces at once (e.g. square or hexagonal heads). [LEC s.12]
- [MIL-F022] Gang milling uses several cutters of the same or different types/diameters mounted together on the arbor to machine several surfaces simultaneously. [LEC s.14; NB p.2]
- [MIL-F023] Gang milling saves machining time and is widely used in repetitive (production) work. [LEC s.14]
- [MIL-F024] The cutting speed for a gang of cutters is calculated from the cutter of the largest diameter. [LEC s.14]
- [MIL-F025] Form milling uses a cutter with specially shaped teeth whose shape matches the required profile; it is used for cutting gear teeth. [LEC s.13]
- [MIL-F026] The cutting rate in form milling is about 20–30% less than in plain milling. [LEC s.13]
- [MIL-F027] Angular milling produces surfaces at an angle (other than 90°) to the spindle axis, e.g. V-blocks. [LEC s.15]
- [MIL-F028] Slot milling cuts slots or keyways using an end mill, slot drill, side-and-face or slitting cutter. [TA s.15–16; theory: P.N. Rao]
- [MIL-F029] Milling operations listed in the lecture: plain, face, end, straddle, form, gang and angular milling. [LEC s.8]

## 4. Milling cutters
- [MIL-F030] A side-and-face cutter has cutting edges on both sides as well as on the periphery, so it can cut on its face and sides. [CHART]
- [MIL-F031] Cutters shown on the lab wall chart: plain, helical plain, side-and-face, interlocking side, half-side and staggered-tooth side milling cutters. [CHART]
- [MIL-F032] A slab (plain) cutter is a large cylindrical cutter with teeth on the outside; it cuts large flat surfaces. [TA s.15]
- [MIL-F033] A slitting (slotting) saw is a thin cutter used to make narrow slots or to cut off. [TA s.15]
- [MIL-F034] A T-slot cutter cuts T-slots such as those in machine tables. [TA s.15]
- [MIL-F035] A hollow milling cutter is pipe-shaped with teeth inside and is used to cut rods to size. [TA s.15]
- [MIL-F036] An end mill cuts on its tip and sides; a ball-nose end mill has a rounded tip for 3-D contouring. [TA s.16]
- [MIL-F037] A face-mill cutter is a large cutter (often with inserts) that cuts with its bottom face to give flat, smooth surfaces. [TA s.16]
- [MIL-F038] A dovetail cutter has angled sides and cuts dovetail grooves. [TA s.16]
- [MIL-F039] A fly cutter is a simple bar with a single cutting tip, used for large smooth flat areas. [TA s.16]
- [MIL-F040] A roughing end mill has wavy/serrated edges and removes a lot of material with a rough finish. [TA s.16]
- [MIL-F041] Form (formed) cutters are shaped to a specific profile and make gears or threads. [TA s.15]
- [MIL-F042] Spur gear teeth in our lab are cut with an involute gear cutter (a form cutter). [TA s.14; TOOLS]
- [MIL-F043] Involute gear cutters are the oldest and most common gear cutters; each cutter in a set covers a range of tooth numbers. [TA s.14]
- [MIL-F044] Milling cutter nomenclature includes: outside diameter, hole (bore) diameter, keyway, face of tooth, land, cutting edge, rake angle, primary and secondary clearance angles, depth of tooth, pitch of teeth, heel, width and helix angle. [LEC s.16]

## 5. Milling machines and parts
- [MIL-F045] Types of milling machine: column-and-knee type (hand, horizontal, vertical, universal), fixed-bed (bed/planer) type, and CNC milling machines. [LEC s.17; TA s.9; NB p.2]
- [MIL-F046] Column-and-knee machines are classed horizontal or vertical according to the axis of the spindle (cutter). [NB p.2]
- [MIL-F047] In a horizontal milling machine the spindle (cutter axis) is horizontal, parallel to the table; cutters are mounted on an arbor. [LEC s.18; TA s.11]
- [MIL-F048] In a vertical milling machine the spindle is vertical, perpendicular to the table; it uses end mills and face mills. [LEC s.19; TA s.11]
- [MIL-F049] Horizontal machines give higher metal-removal rate (heavy cuts, large flat surfaces); vertical machines are more versatile for slots, holes and complex profiles. [TA s.11]
- [MIL-F050] Main parts of a horizontal column-and-knee milling machine: base, column, knee, saddle, table, spindle, arbor, overarm (overhanging arm) and arbor support. [LEC s.18; CHART]
- [MIL-F051] Wall-chart parts of the column-and-knee milling machine: base, elevating screw, knee, knee elevating handle, cross-feed handle, saddle, table, front brace, arbor support, arbor, overhanging arm, cutter, column, cone pulley, telescopic feed shaft. [CHART]
- [MIL-F052] The column is the main vertical casting mounted on the base; it houses the spindle drive and carries the overarm. [theory: Hajra Choudhury]
- [MIL-F053] The knee slides up and down on the column (vertical feed) by the elevating screw; it carries the saddle and table. [CHART; theory: Hajra Choudhury]
- [MIL-F054] The saddle moves on the knee towards or away from the column (cross feed); the table moves on the saddle longitudinally. [theory: Hajra Choudhury]
- [MIL-F055] The arbor holds the cutters on a horizontal machine; it is supported at its outer end by the arbor support hanging from the overarm. [CHART; theory: Hajra Choudhury]
- [MIL-F056] The vertical milling machine has a milling head and a ram (overarm); its parts include the head, ram, column, spindle, quill, table, saddle and knee. [LEC s.19; TA s.10]
- [MIL-F057] In a bed-type milling machine, the work table is mounted directly on a large bed (replacing the knee) and moves only horizontally. [LEC s.20]
- [MIL-F058] Our lab uses a universal horizontal (column-and-knee type) milling machine for the gear job. [MLS1; LS p.9]

## 6. Indexing and dividing head
- [MIL-F059] Indexing is dividing the periphery of a workpiece into a number of equal (or unequal) divisions, by rotating the work by a precise angle between cuts. [LEC s.22; TA s.17]
- [MIL-F060] An index plate is a circular plate with several circles of equally spaced holes; the index pin engages these holes to control crank movement. [LEC s.22; TA s.17]
- [MIL-F061] Parts of the universal dividing head (wall chart): work spindle, worm wheel, worm, shaft, gears, index plate, index crank, latch pin, tailstock, swivel block, body, sector (arms). [CHART]
- [MIL-F062] The worm wheel of the dividing head has 40 teeth and the worm is single-start, giving a 40 : 1 ratio. [LEC s.22, s.25]
- [MIL-F063] 40 turns of the index crank rotate the work spindle (job) once. [NB p.3; LEC s.25]
- [MIL-F064] Indexing methods: direct, simple (plain), compound and differential indexing. [LEC s.23]
- [MIL-F065] Direct (rapid) indexing is the simplest: the worm is disengaged and the spindle plate is turned by hand; it uses a direct plate with 24, 30 or 36 holes. [LEC s.23]
- [MIL-F066] Direct-indexing example: for a square (4 divisions) choose the 24-hole plate (smallest divisible) and move 24/4 = 6 holes each time. [LEC s.24]
- [MIL-F067] Simple indexing uses the worm and worm wheel (40:1); crank turns per division = 40/N. [LEC s.25; NB p.3; TA s.19]
- [MIL-F068] Simple-indexing example: N = 7 → 40/7 = 5 5/7 = 5 full turns + 35 holes on the 49-hole circle. [LEC s.25–26]
- [MIL-F069] Compound indexing combines a crank movement on one hole circle with a movement of the plate itself on another circle (rear lock pin released). [LEC s.27]
- [MIL-F070] Differential indexing (gearing between spindle and plate) is used when simple indexing cannot give the division; the dividing-head chart shows set-ups for differential indexing and helical milling. [CHART; theory: Hajra Choudhury]
- [MIL-F071] Brown & Sharpe index plates used in our lab — Plate 1: 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20; Plate 2: 21, 23, 27, 29, 31, 33; Plate 3: 37, 39, 41, 43, 47, 49 holes. [NB p.4; MLS2; TA s.18; BOARD]
- [MIL-F072] The sector arms are set to span the required number of holes so the count need not be repeated each time. [theory: Hajra Choudhury]
- [MIL-F073] The index crank must not be turned back (opposite direction) during indexing, otherwise backlash spoils the division. [MLS1]

## 7. Gear terminology and formulas (inch, diametral pitch system)
- [MIL-F074] Diametral pitch DP = number of teeth per inch of pitch-circle diameter: DP = N / D_p (D_p in inches). [TA s.19; theory: Machinery's Handbook] (corrects notebook)
- [MIL-F075] Outside (blank) diameter OD = (N + 2) / DP. [NB p.3; BOARD; MLS2; TA s.19]
- [MIL-F076] Pitch-circle diameter D_p = N / DP. [theory: Machinery's Handbook]
- [MIL-F077] Addendum = 1/DP; dedendum = 1.157/DP; clearance = 0.157/DP. [theory: Machinery's Handbook]
- [MIL-F078] Whole depth of tooth = addendum + dedendum = 2.157/DP; standard working depth = 2/DP. [theory: Machinery's Handbook]
- [MIL-F079] In our lab the depth set for cutting the gear teeth, called "working depth (W.D.)", is 2.157/DP (textbooks call 2.157/DP the whole depth; questions on the lab job accept the lab name and never offer 2/DP as a distractor). [BOARD; MLS2; NB p.4; TA s.19]
- [MIL-F080] Circular pitch = π/DP; tooth thickness on the pitch circle = π/(2·DP). [theory: Machinery's Handbook]
- [MIL-F081] Module (mm) = 25.4 / DP; module = pitch-circle diameter (mm) / N. [theory: Machinery's Handbook]
- [MIL-F082] A spur gear is the simplest and most common gear; its teeth are straight and parallel to the axis of rotation. [TA s.19]
- [MIL-F083] Spur gear nomenclature: pitch circle, addendum circle (outside circle), dedendum (root) circle, addendum, dedendum, circular pitch, tooth thickness, face width, working depth, whole depth, clearance. [theory: Machinery's Handbook]

## 8. Gear types
- [MIL-F084] Spur and helical gears connect parallel shafts. [theory: Machinery's Handbook]
- [MIL-F085] Helical gears have teeth inclined to the axis; they run more smoothly and quietly than spur gears but produce axial thrust. [theory: Machinery's Handbook]
- [MIL-F086] Double-helical (herringbone) gears have two opposite helices, which cancel the axial thrust; used on parallel shafts. [theory: Machinery's Handbook]
- [MIL-F087] Bevel gears connect intersecting shafts (usually at 90°). [theory: Machinery's Handbook]
- [MIL-F088] Worm and worm wheel connect non-parallel, non-intersecting shafts at 90° and give a large speed reduction (as in the dividing head, 40:1). [theory: Machinery's Handbook; LEC s.22]
- [MIL-F089] Rack and pinion convert rotary motion into straight-line (linear) motion; the rack is a gear of infinite radius. [theory: Machinery's Handbook]
- [MIL-F090] In an internal gear the teeth are cut on the inside of a ring; it meshes with an external pinion on parallel shafts. [theory: Machinery's Handbook]
- [MIL-F091] Crossed helical (spiral) gears connect non-parallel, non-intersecting shafts. [theory: Machinery's Handbook]

## 9. The ME-106 milling job
- [MIL-F092] Objective of the milling job: to make a spur gear by milling from a circular workpiece. [LS p.9; MLS1; BOARD]
- [MIL-F093] The milling workpiece is an aluminium circular disc (blank with a centre hole). [NB p.2; LS p.9]
- [MIL-F094] The diametral pitch used for the lab gear is DP = 16 (teeth per inch). [NB p.3; BOARD; MLS2]
- [MIL-F095] Practice example (earlier batch, not our job): OD = 1 in, DP = 16 → N = 16 × 1 − 2 = 14 teeth. [NB p.3; LS p.9]
- [MIL-F096] Practice example: indexing for 14 teeth = 40/14 = 2 6/7 = 2 full turns + 18 holes on the 21-hole circle (plate 2). [NB p.3–4]
- [MIL-F097] The ME-106 gear job (confirmed current batch): OD = 1½ in, DP = 16 → N = 16 × 1.5 − 2 = 22 teeth. [BOARD; MLS2]
- [MIL-F098] Indexing for the ME-106 22-tooth gear: 40/22 = 1 9/11 = 1 full turn + 27 holes on the 33-hole circle (plate 2). [BOARD; MLS2]
- [MIL-F099] Depth for DP 16: 2.157/16 = 0.1348 in ≈ 3.42 mm (≈ 3.4 mm). [BOARD; MLS2; NB p.4] (board's 0.1328 in is an arithmetic slip — see corrections)
- [MIL-F100] The gear blank is mounted on a mandrel (shaft) held between the dividing head and tailstock. [LS p.9; MLS1; NB p.2]

## 11. Procedure (our lab)
- [MIL-F105] Step 1: calculate the number of teeth from the outside diameter (OD) and diametral pitch (DP). [LS p.9; MLS1]
- [MIL-F106] Step 2: calculate the working depth (2.157/DP) and the indexing (40/N). [MLS1]
- [MIL-F107] Step 3: fix the appropriate index plate (with the needed hole circle) on the dividing head. [MLS1]
- [MIL-F108] Step 4: clamp the workpiece (on the mandrel) tightly in the machine; switch on the machine and coolant. [MLS1; LS p.9]
- [MIL-F109] Step 5: slowly feed the table so the blank meets the cutter and one tooth space is cut; bring the table back, index the crank, and repeat until all teeth are cut. [MLS1; LS p.9]
- [MIL-F110] Step 6: finish (polish/deburr) the gear with a file. [MLS1; LS p.9]

## 12. Precautions (milling shop)
- [MIL-F111] Wear proper (safety) shoes and an apron. [MLS1; LS p.9; TA s.2]
- [MIL-F112] Maintain a proper distance from the machine while it is running. [MLS1; LS p.9]
- [MIL-F113] Fix the workpiece in the machine properly (tightly). [MLS1]
- [MIL-F114] Do not rotate the index-plate handle (crank) in the opposite direction. [MLS1]
- [MIL-F115] Stay alert during fixing and cutting. [MLS1; LS p.9; TA s.2]
- [MIL-F116] Do not touch any electrical connections. [LS p.9]
- [MIL-F117] Avoid loose threads, ornaments and wristwatches while working. [TA s.2]
- [MIL-F118] Do not operate the machine without supervision of the shop instructor; clean the machine and workspace after use. [TA s.2]
- [MIL-F119] Never remove chips or measure the job while the cutter is rotating. [theory: Hajra Choudhury]

## 13. Advantages, applications
- [MIL-F120] Milling gives high metal-removal rates (multi-point cutter) and can produce flat surfaces, slots, keyways, gears, splines and contours. [LEC s.3; TA s.6]
- [MIL-F121] Applications of milling include gear cutting, keyways, slots, flat surfaces of machine parts and dies. [TA s.15–16; theory: P.N. Rao]

## 14. Standard milling practice (set-up, cutting and safety)
- [MIL-F122] Brown & Sharpe involute cutter numbers: No. 1 = 135 teeth to rack, No. 2 = 55–134, No. 3 = 35–54, No. 4 = 26–34, No. 5 = 21–25, No. 6 = 17–20, No. 7 = 14–16, No. 8 = 12–13 teeth. [theory: Machinery's Handbook]
- [MIL-F123] The gear cutter is centred over the axis of the blank before cutting, so the tooth spaces are symmetrical. [theory: Hajra Choudhury]
- [MIL-F124] To set the depth, the rotating cutter is brought down to just touch the top of the blank (e.g. with a paper strip), the knee dial is set to zero, the work is moved clear, and the knee is raised by the calculated depth. [theory: Hajra Choudhury]
- [MIL-F125] The dividing head and tailstock centres must be aligned with the table travel, and the blank must be tight on the mandrel. [theory: Hajra Choudhury]
- [MIL-F126] After setting the depth, the knee and saddle are locked so only the table (longitudinal) feed moves during the cut. [theory: Hajra Choudhury]
- [MIL-F127] Never reach over or near the rotating cutter; keep hands away from the cutter path. [theory: Hajra Choudhury]
- [MIL-F128] Stop the machine before measuring the job, removing chips or adjusting the work. [theory: Hajra Choudhury]
- [MIL-F129] Check that the cutter rotates in the correct direction and that the arbor nut is tightened only after the arbor support is in place. [theory: Hajra Choudhury]
- [MIL-F130] Wear safety goggles while milling; chips fly from the cutter. [theory: Hajra Choudhury]
- [MIL-F131] Keep the cutter guard in position and use the correct cutter speed and feed for the material. [theory: Hajra Choudhury]
